Heart

Immersive WebGL narrative · personal cinematic web experience

Eight full-screen acts, each its own real-time WebGL scene — procedural particle systems, custom shaders, film scenes, generated terrain — stitched into one continuous, transition-linked journey that holds up on a phone. The narrative is personal and stays offline; what’s shown here is the engineering.

Built on vanilla Three.js — deliberately not react-three-fiber — to keep hand-tuned shader and timing work off a reconciler. A central section sequencer mounts exactly one WebGL context at a time: each act owns its full lifecycle (context, rAF loop, listeners, audio, geometries, composer) and tears all of it down on unmount — verified zero leaked contexts across the whole run.

No two acts are built alike. A lyric-synced music act samples its palette from the cover art on screen; a scroll-driven interactive scrapbook composites layered paper, pressed flowers and taped polaroids; a particle field dollies into a DRACO-compressed GLTF landscape; five depth-map 2.5D parallax scenes each carry their own typographic identity; one config-driven engine generates every terrain scene from real DEM elevation tiles; and four particle acts share a single renderer.

Custom GLSL throughout: domain-warped fbm nebulae, HSL-in-shader particle coloring, Fresnel and flat-facet materials, particle-text formed from glyph silhouettes. GSAP + ScrollTrigger paces the run, and act transitions are synthesized live with the Web Audio API — no sound asset.

Favorite bug: dense additive-blend particles saturated to a pure-white blob. The fix was systematic — cut density, raise the bloom threshold, and clamp gl_PointSize, since perspective point sizing balloons near-camera points into a solid mass. Tuning was “measure, don’t eyeball”: k-means palettes from reference photos, numeric color-grade checks, ffmpeg volumedetect for audio levels.

Connected Teddy-Bear Workshop Platform

Client work · funded product · sole developer

For a teddy-bear workshop business: customers build a physical bear in-store, receive a unique character code, and redeem it in a companion app where their exact bear — type, color, clothing, accessories — comes to life in a top-down 2D life sim built in Unity.

The sim has grown well past dress-up. Bears have needs, five skills and a personality; you farm crops through real server-clocked growth stages, chop and cook what you harvest, craft from gathered materials, keep bees, dye and wear clothing across six equipment slots, run a market stall, train at sports and go to school. There are nine minigames, six of them with a harder timed event behind a medal, and a 336-question school bank across four subjects, four years and three difficulty bands that adapts without ever showing a child a score.

I own the whole stack for a confidential European client: the Unity client, a Node/Express API over Supabase/PostgreSQL, and a React admin panel for store staff. The economy layer is built defensively — a coin ledger with earned/purchased separation (spends debit earned first, so the purchased balance reads as unspent real-money liability), a DB-trigger-enforced order state machine (physical merchandise ships on the strength of it, so the UI doesn’t get a vote), idempotent starter-bear redemption (every account plays immediately; redeeming a real code rewrites the same row, and a second code adopts the shop-built bear as another character rather than replacing it), and an Apple 3.1.1-compliant split — digital items are coin-priced, physical goods are card checkout only, never IAP currency. A reviewer pool for App Store submission rides on the same partition: a flagged account’s physical order can never reach shipped, enforced in the transition trigger rather than in a route.

Three code namespaces, partitioned in the schema rather than by convention — the uppercase rule in the app is what makes the partition unspellable, and a CHECK is what makes it true.

Correctness is checked by five independent harnesses, because each is blind to something the next one sees: 698 unit tests on the pure logic, 260 assertions on what a built scene contains, 404 API tests, all 69 migrations replayed against an empty Postgres — and 30 headless play scenarios that boot the real game, sign in against a live API, walk the bear across the map, press the button a child presses, and photograph every screen. That last one is the only thing that can catch a DTO whose fields have drifted from the JSON the server really sends.

Sacred Trinity — Connect Baltimore

Lead engineer 2025, technical lead & mentor 2026 · DALI Lab · environmental justice · live

An environmental-justice platform for Baltimore City built at the DALI Lab with the Baltimore Social-Environmental Collaborative. It organizes three community stakeholders — faith leaders, research institutions, and K-12 public schools — around neighborhood-scale climate and pollution data. Each “Sacred Trinity” is a church, its partner school, and the forest patch between them.

In 2025 I led the first build: a PostgreSQL backend behind 10K+ community and environmental data points with indexing tuned for the map’s hot paths, a React + TypeScript map of district polygons and site markers with per-site time series, and weather and pollution API integrations with a caching layer that cut load latency ~40%, behind JWT/OAuth2 auth with rate limiting and audit logging.

In fall 2026 I came back to lead the next version and mentor two developers. The map now carries the full 65-station network — churches, schools, gardens, libraries, universities — on MapLibre with heat, redlining and canopy overlays, and every site gets a health score built from nine public datasets (air, tree canopy, sewer overflows, blood lead, crime, 311), framed on the city’s Children’s Outdoor Bill of Rights, with every input traceable to its source and last-updated date. Seeding the station network also caught five production sensors wired to the wrong device.

  • 9 public datasets behind one health score per site

Gift of Health — Offline-First EMR

Humanity First · Lead Full-Stack Developer

Medical-mission clinics can’t count on internet, so their records system shouldn’t either. I’m leading a ground-up rebuild of Humanity First’s offline-first EMR: one Windows laptop runs the whole stack, and doctors, nurses, and students work on it from their phones and laptops over the clinic’s local Wi-Fi or a hotspot.

The old Streamlit prototype had shared PINs and plaintext JSON files. The new system puts every read and write through one permission-checked, audited service layer. Each staff member has their own account. Students can be switched on or off for each trip. Every patient-record view is written to a tamper-evident, HMAC-chained audit log that the database itself won’t let the app edit. Patient intake, vitals, diagnoses, prescriptions, a doctor queue for each specialty, and photo uploads with metadata stripped all run with no internet connection.

  • Offline on a LAN or hotspot, many devices at once
  • Role-based access enforced on the server, past trips read-only
  • Audit log with an HMAC hash chain and an append-only DB role
  • Tested with 270+ unit, integration & browser tests in CI
